Why We Get Stuck Solving Problems

The real obstacle is rarely the problem itself — it's the way we frame it in the first place.

Every engineer, at some point in their career, encounters a problem that refuses to yield. The equations are correct. The logic is sound. The approach is methodical. And yet, the solution remains stubbornly out of reach. It is tempting, in these moments, to blame the problem — to assume it is too complex, too novel, too difficult. But more often than not, the obstacle is not the problem. The obstacle is us. Specifically, it is the way we have chosen to think about the problem — the frame we have placed around it before we even begin to solve it.

The Tyranny of the First Approach

When confronted with a problem, the human mind instinctively reaches for a framework. We categorise. We analogise. We ask, "What does this look like?" and we match the new problem to an old pattern. This is, in many ways, an efficient cognitive strategy. It allows us to navigate a complex world without starting from zero each time. But it carries a hidden cost: the first framework we choose often becomes a prison. Once we have decided what the problem looks like, we stop seeing what it actually is.

This phenomenon has a name in psychology: functional fixedness. It describes the inability to see an object or concept as anything other than its conventional use. The classic example is Duncker's candle problem: given a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and a book of matches, how do you attach the candle to the wall so it can burn without dripping wax on the floor? Most people struggle because they see the box as a container for the thumbtacks, not as a shelf. The solution — tacking the box to the wall and placing the candle on it — requires seeing the box differently. It requires breaking the frame.

Einstellung: The Expert's Trap

There is a cruel irony in expertise: the more skilled you become at solving a particular type of problem, the more likely you are to apply that solution to problems where it does not belong. This is the Einstellung effect — the tendency for a known solution to block the discovery of a better one. Chess players fall prey to it when they see a familiar pattern and execute a well-rehearsed response, missing a shorter or more elegant path. Engineers fall prey to it when they default to the tools and methods they have mastered, even when the problem demands something unfamiliar.

The Einstellung effect is particularly dangerous because it feels like competence. You are not idle. You are working hard, applying your skills, progressing along a path. But the path is wrong, and the further you travel along it, the harder it becomes to turn back. This is why some of the most significant breakthroughs in engineering have come from outsiders — people who lacked the expertise to know which approaches were "supposed" to work, and therefore approached the problem with fresh eyes.

The Power of Reframing

The most effective problem-solvers are not always the most knowledgeable. They are the ones who know when to stop solving and start reframing. Reframing is the act of stepping back from the problem and asking: "Am I solving the right problem?" It sounds simple. It is extraordinarily difficult.

Consider the apocryphal story of NASA and the space pen. The story goes that NASA spent millions developing a pen that could write in zero gravity, while the Soviets simply used a pencil. The story is largely inaccurate — the Fisher Space Pen was developed privately, and pencils posed genuine safety risks in spacecraft — but the principle it illustrates is real. Sometimes the most elegant solution is not a better answer to the existing question, but a different question entirely.

In engineering practice, reframing often takes the form of questioning constraints. Many problems are difficult not because they are inherently complex, but because they contain assumptions that have been treated as fixed. "The beam must be steel." "The system must run in real time." "The user must interact through a screen." These constraints may be genuine, or they may be inherited habits of thought. The act of interrogating them — asking "why?" with the persistence of a child — often reveals that the problem is smaller, or at least differently shaped, than it first appeared.

Walking Away as Strategy

There is a reason solutions often arrive in the shower, on a walk, or in the moments before sleep. The conscious mind, when intensely focused on a problem, narrows its attention. It tightens its grip on the frame it has chosen and examines details more and more closely. The subconscious mind, freed from the tyranny of focus, wanders. It makes associations the conscious mind would dismiss as irrelevant. It connects disparate ideas. It sees the box as a shelf.

This is not laziness. It is cognitive strategy. The deliberate act of walking away from a problem — genuinely disengaging, not merely worrying about it in a different room — allows the brain to process information in a fundamentally different way. The breakthrough feels sudden when it arrives, but it is the product of background computation that began the moment you stopped trying.

First Principles Thinking

Perhaps the most powerful antidote to cognitive stuckness is the discipline of first principles thinking. Rather than reasoning by analogy — "this problem is like this other problem, so the solution must be similar" — first principles thinking asks you to decompose the problem to its most fundamental truths and build upward from there.

Elon Musk has famously described this approach in the context of battery costs: rather than accepting the market price of batteries as a given, he asked what raw materials constitute a battery, what those materials cost, and whether a battery could be assembled more cheaply from components. The answer was yes, and the result was a fundamental shift in the economics of electric vehicles.

First principles thinking is slow, effortful, and often uncomfortable. It requires abandoning the safety of precedent and accepted wisdom. But it is also the only reliable method for solving problems that existing frameworks cannot accommodate.

The most dangerous words in engineering are not "I don't know." They are "We've always done it this way." The first is an invitation to learn. The second is a refusal.

Getting unstuck is not about working harder. It is about working differently — about developing the humility to admit that your frame may be wrong, the courage to abandon a familiar approach, and the patience to sit with uncertainty while a new approach forms. The problem, almost always, is solvable. The question is whether you are willing to see it as it is, rather than as you assumed it to be.
